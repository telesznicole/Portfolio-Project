## Your Answers
Locked 2026-08-XX.

"Everything built across the three pages needed to actually prove itself, not just exist. A final validation study with real UX practitioners tested whether it did.

Ten practitioners tested the finished site and rated their own confidence before and after, on three things: recognizing exclusion in design, considering diverse users, and applying inclusive methods to their own work. Every measure increased.

The strongest shift was in recognizing exclusion, jumping from 74% to 88% of maximum confidence, the clearest sign the simulation actually did its job. One participant put it simply: it 'made me think deeper than I think I would have normally.' Confidence in considering diverse users and in applying inclusive methods both grew too, landing at 92% and 84%, already strong starting points that still moved further.

[STAT BANNER: 74% → 88% recognizing exclusion / 92% considering diverse users / 84% applying inclusive methods]

The forward-looking numbers were even stronger. Practitioners rated their anticipated positive impact from applying these approaches at 96%, and their own readiness to actually integrate inclusive patterns going forward at 92%. People didn't just understand the framework. They said they'd use it.

[STAT BANNER: 96% anticipated positive impact / 92% readiness to integrate]

The implications reach past the study itself. Participants' own responses point toward something bigger: a shift away from purely neutral, efficiency-first defaults and toward genuinely intentional, inclusive decision-making, one that starts with individual designers having better tools, not waiting for it to be handed down from above."

Closing line:
"Ten sessions can't prove behavior change on their own. But they show something real: people walked away more confident, more aware, and genuinely ready to bring these ideas into their own work."

## Revision History

- **Numbers presented as percentage of maximum possible score (score ÷ 5), not raw 1–5 scale or relative percentage increase.** Both alternatives were considered and rejected: raw scale numbers ("4.5 → 4.6") read as unimpressive to a reader unfamiliar with the scale, and relative percentage increase made the two smaller, still-genuinely-strong gains (considering diverse users, applying inclusive methods) look weak (roughly +2% each) even though their absolute confidence levels were already high. Percentage-of-maximum reads consistently strong across all five real numbers, sourced directly from the thesis's own Pre-/Post-Survey Analysis section (verified against the canonical document directly, not secondhand from any draft or Miro material, per the Content-Sourcing Priority Principle).
- **A real, substantive error caught in the first draft's opener**: "measures the whole project was built to move" was vague filler that didn't actually communicate what was measured. Rewritten to state directly what the three real measures were.
- **A more serious error caught in an early paragraph framing the smallest of the three confidence gains (82% → 84%, applying inclusive methods) as something to "state plainly rather than hide."** This mis-applied Meta's "honest room to grow" pattern to data that didn't actually call for it: Meta's version worked because 60% wanting more AI adaptation was a genuinely separate, distinct finding. Here, all three numbers land in a strong 74–92% range, and singling out the smallest as a confession implied a weakness that isn't actually present in the data. Rewritten to present all three gains together, in the same positive framing, with no singled-out "weak point."
- **The field-implications paragraph, pulled from the thesis's own closing "Field and Stakeholder Implications" section, was caught overreaching twice and corrected both times.** First version claimed "a shift the whole UX field is due for" — too sweeping a claim for the study's actual scope. Revised to frame it as what participants' own responses point toward, not a standalone claim about the entire field.
- **The closing line went through the most significant revision in this section.** The original version claimed the site is "something people actually use and want to keep using" — a real overreach Nicole caught directly, since the thesis's own methodology is explicit that this measures self-reported confidence, not actual behavioral change or ongoing usage. Rewritten to state exactly what the data supports: increased confidence and readiness, not proven adoption or use.
- **The real participant quote (`'made me think deeper than I think I would have normally'`) was kept woven directly into the prose**, not set apart in its own card, per the format decision above.
