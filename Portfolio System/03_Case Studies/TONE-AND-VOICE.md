# Tone & Voice Guide

> Established 2026-08-21, during Meta's Context section. This applies across all case studies (Meta, Feminist UX, future projects) — the goal is one consistent voice throughout the portfolio, not a per-project tone.

---

## The core principle: specificity beats formality

The instinct to sound "less academic" usually leads to adding casualness (contractions, looser sentence structure). That's not actually the right lever. **What reads as academic/stiff is abstraction, not formality.** Concrete, specific phrasing is *both* more credible and more natural-sounding than vague phrasing, regardless of how formal the sentence structure around it is.

- ❌ Abstract: *"core to the product's future relevance"*
- ✅ Concrete: *"they hadn't yet become part of how Gen Z actually connects with each other day to day"*

When a sentence feels stiff, the fix is almost always "what specifically happened," not "make the words smaller."

## Register

Balanced — professional credibility with real personality, not corporate-clean and not casual. Landed through iteration: too casual felt like it undercut credibility; too clean/report-like felt academic and impersonal. The target sits between those two, closer to natural spoken confidence than either extreme.

## Sentence rhythm

Vary sentence length deliberately. A few short, punchy sentences mixed with longer ones reads as natural and confident. A run of same-length, evenly-hedged sentences is a tell of over-smoothing — break it up.

## Avoid — AI-writing tells

- **Minimize em dashes.** Use colons, periods, or restructure instead. (Not a hard zero, but default away from them.)
- Avoid stacking three parallel items in one clause when it makes the sentence run long — split into two sentences instead of cramming with commas.
- Avoid empty intensifiers and hedge-y qualifiers that don't add real information ("truly," "genuinely," "significant" without a concrete referent).

## Person / voice — "I" vs "we"

Established framework (2026-08-21): the tension between showing individual proof-of-skill and honestly crediting a 22-person team collaboration resolves by *function*, not by picking one pronoun for the whole piece:

- **Use "I"** for Nicole's specific decisions, judgment calls, and individual actions — this is where hiring managers are evaluating personal contribution, and vague "we" language here actively undermines the case study's purpose.
- **Use "we / the team"** for genuinely collective moments — group presentations, shared pivots, team-wide outcomes.
- Used well, the two reinforce each other: "I" claims real individual agency, "we" shows honest collaboration-crediting, and together they read as more trustworthy than either alone.

**Section-by-section default:**
- **Context** — team-framed by nature (it's the situation *before* Nicole's individual involvement even starts, per the section's own definition), light personal touch is fine ("myself included") but this section shouldn't lean heavily on "I"
- **Discovery, Decisions, Retrospective** — "I" should show up clearly and often; this is where personal judgment and process are literally what's being evaluated
- **Constraints, Production, Outcome** — case-by-case; use "I" when describing a personal call, "we" for team/external facts

## Calibration example

The finalized Context paragraph (below) is the reference point for tone going forward. If a later section's draft feels off, compare it against this one rather than re-deriving the rules from scratch. (Updated 2026-08-23 to match Context's final approved text — see Meta's `01_Content/01-context.md` for the full revision history.)

> *"In early 2025, Meta partnered with SCADpro, bringing on a team of 22 students, myself included, to help build toward its own mission: the future of human connection, and the technology that makes it possible. The Ray-Ban Meta glasses were strong technology, but they hadn't yet become part of how Gen Z actually connects with each other day to day. The brief came with real constraints already attached: no changes to the hardware, and a solution achievable within 18 to 24 months. This wasn't a concept exercise. It needed to be something Meta could realistically build."*

## Additional lessons from revision (2026-08-21)

- **Ordering matters as much as word choice.** A closing payoff line should pay off whatever sits directly before it — don't let an unrelated point get wedged between a setup and its payoff, even if that point is true and well-written on its own.
- **Watch for "the brief/report/document made clear" style phrasing** — this abstracts the sentence's subject away from any real person or action, which is a specific, nameable version of the "too academic" problem. Prefer tying the sentence to what was actually being solved or done.
