## Context
**Status:** Text ready

In early 2025, Meta partnered with SCADpro, bringing on a team of 22 students, myself included, to help build toward its own mission: the future of human connection, and the technology that makes it possible. The Ray-Ban Meta glasses were strong technology, but they hadn't yet become part of how Gen Z actually connects with each other day to day. The brief came with real constraints already attached: no changes to the hardware, and a solution achievable within 18 to 24 months. This wasn't a concept exercise. It needed to be something Meta could realistically build.

## Discovery
**Status:** Text ready — structured as opener + 4 finding cards + closing reframe; supporting images optional, not yet picked

Opener:
My work on this project started with research: self-ethnography, interviews with friends, and online research, on top of structured focus groups and surveys, pulling in 150+ survey responses and 300+ insights from focus group sessions alone, totaling more than 5,000 data points. I contributed across that data collection, but where I focused most was synthesis, turning all of it into affinity-mapped themes.

Finding 1:
Public use came with real friction. Several participants described feeling self-conscious triggering voice commands like "Hey Meta" around other people.

Finding 2:
People wanted the AI itself to feel less robotic. A recurring wish across interviews was for a more natural, humanized interaction, not just a faster assistant.

Finding 3:
Self-expression mattered more than expected, but the hardware itself was locked. With 71% seeing wearable tech as self-expression, that pushed personalization toward the experience layer instead of the product.

Finding 4:
Belonging mattered as much as personalization. 76% said they only shared with their inner circle, treating connection as something built in small circles, not broadcast widely.

Closing reframe:
Across all of it, we landed on a three-tier hierarchy of what Gen Z actually wanted: for the glasses to fit seamlessly into everyday life, to create small moments of shared belonging, and to feel like a genuine extension of personal identity. That wasn't a feature gap. It was a behavior gap. People didn't need the glasses to do more, they needed a way to use them that fit naturally into how they already moved through the world.

## Constraints
**Status:** Text ready — structured as opener + 4 constraint cards + closing line, matching Discovery's card format

Opener:
Once we knew this was a behavior problem, not a feature problem, we still had to work inside real limits on how we could respond.

Constraint 1:
Coordinating a team of 22 also meant deciding how to explore that many directions at once. We split into focused sub-teams so each could dig into a different angle in parallel, then bring what we found back together.

Constraint 2:
Every proposed feature got mapped against development time, novelty, and complexity, sorted into three tiers: immediately actionable, possibly feasible, and most ambitious. That framework shaped which ideas we pushed further and which we set aside early.

Constraint 3:
Some limits showed up mid-process when someone from Meta stress-tested our concepts to try to break them. One gesture-based idea didn't hold up: recognizing custom gestures needed the camera running almost continuously, draining battery too fast to be practical.

Constraint 4:
Building a real, working AI backend wasn't realistic in this timeframe either, so we used Wizard-of-Oz prototyping: real LLM responses synced to sound cues timed to play through the glasses, letting us test genuine reactions before investing in infrastructure we didn't need yet.

Closing line:
Together, these constraints didn't close the door on ambition. They just made clear exactly where the real decisions needed to happen.

## Decisions
**Status:** Text ready — uses visible redaction bar (▓) where final concept name would go; structured as opener + 4 decision cards (each: bold claim line + alternative/reason line) + closing line

Opener:
With those constraints in place, and after narrowing nearly 1,000 raw ideas down to about 80 real directions, a handful of decisions ended up shaping where the project actually went.

Decision 1 (claim):
I proposed ▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓▓ as a way to unify three directions our research had converged toward, deeper capture tools, personalized modes, and shared-memory features, into one connected concept instead of three competing pitches.
Decision 1 (alternative/reason):
The team had been developing those three directions separately. Presenting them as standalone features would have diluted the pitch into a list rather than one coherent product direction, and none of them alone addressed the full behavior gap we'd found in research.

Decision 2 (claim):
We chose to build out the full end-to-end platform experience instead of continuing to refine our individual example concepts in isolation.
Decision 2 (alternative/reason):
The real alternative was to keep polishing each concept further before moving on. Client feedback made clear that a cohesive platform story mattered more at this stage than perfecting any single feature in isolation.

Decision 3 (claim):
We built a three-part framework, feasibility, relevance, and alignment with the broader vision, to choose our core direction, instead of deciding by instinct or consensus alone.
Decision 3 (alternative/reason):
We could have kept multiple directions alive longer and let the client narrow them down for us. We chose to make and defend the call ourselves, since a framework we could walk stakeholders through would hold up better under real scrutiny.

Decision 4 (claim):
We validated our direction through structured user testing before committing further resources to it.
Decision 4 (alternative/reason):
We could have moved straight from concept to final production on client and internal confidence alone. We chose to test with real users first, since that's the only way to catch blind spots before they got expensive to fix.

Closing line:
None of these decisions were free. Choosing one direction meant walking away from others that were just as real.

## Deep/Technical
**Status:** Text ready — 3 collapsible cards

Card 1 — Coordinating 22 People Across 13 Disciplines:
The team spanned UX, industrial design, sound design, film, advertising, and more, 13 majors in total. Rather than trying to run one unified process across all of it, we split into focused sub-teams to explore different directions in parallel, each with the freedom to dig deep on its own angle, then regularly brought everything back together to compare notes and converge.

Card 2 — The Numbers Behind the Narrowing:
Nearly 1,000 raw ideas came out of early ideation. That got filtered to around 80 real directions, then grouped into a handful of thematic categories, before finally converging on one. Each stage of that funnel used a different method, round-robin ideation, a novelty-and-relevancy matrix, affinity mapping, so the narrowing was deliberate at every step, not just a gut-check.

Card 3 — Simulating AI Without Building It:
A working AI backend wasn't realistic to build in this timeframe, so we simulated it by hand. Real LLM responses were paired with sound cues played out loud at the exact moment a user would expect a reaction, letting us act out and test full interactions in real time instead of just talking through the ideas. Getting that timing right took real coordination, since even a half-second delay broke the illusion.
